\title{\textbf{TR-2026-06}\\[0.3em]
Capability Measures the Driving, Not the Engine\\[0.2em]
\large A three-tier result dissolved, and a defect found in the author's own engine}
\author{Noah Parsons\\[0.2em]
\small ORCID 0009-0000-7224-6040}
\date{5 September 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The frozen scaling sweep reported that three rigid-body dynamics engines answer
the planar $N$-link chain up to $5$, $12$ and $30$ links respectively, and this
was read as a three-tier capability separation. It is not one. Driving each
engine along a different path through the same public interface, and adjudicating
every result against the same closed-form reference, all three reach $30$ --- the
top of the ladder. The reported tiers measured three different driving styles.
Pursuing MechanicsDSL further found two further limits, neither of them
inherent. $97\%$ of compilation was spent in one \code{sympy.simplify} call
whose result is cosmetic; removing it made the engine $37\times$ faster at
$N=8$. What then blocked it at $N=32$ was CPython's default recursion limit ---
an interpreter default, not a property of the problem. Raised, the engine
derives a \textbf{100-coordinate system} in $4414$\,s, agreeing with the
reference to $1.3\times10^{-12}$. Pushing only one engine would have introduced a
fresh asymmetry favouring the author's own, so all three were then asked for
the same systems under the same clock in one session: \textbf{all three reach $80$
coordinates}, and reach is not a differentiator between them at all. The engine already intended to skip the costly call under a
deadline and could not, because on Windows that deadline was inert and its
expiry was raised on a thread where no caller could catch it. Three defects are
fixed here. \textbf{Every measurement in this report agreed with the closed-form
reference}, so nothing here bears on correctness --- only on reach, and on
controls that reported working while doing nothing.
\end{abstract}

% ===========================================================================
\section{Why this was run}
% ===========================================================================

TR-2026-04 reported a scaling ladder in which \code{sympy.physics.mechanics}
answered chains up to $5$ links, MechanicsDSL up to $12$, and Drake up to $30$,
each under a $120$\,s wall clock. The natural reading --- three tiers of
capability --- was carried into the paper's abstract.

That reading has a weakness which a reviewer would find before a reader does.
Each engine was driven along exactly one path, and for SymPy that path was the
one its documentation presents: build a \code{LagrangesMethod} and call
\code{rhs()}. Internally \code{rhs()} evaluates
\code{mass\_matrix\_full.LUsolve(forcing\_full)}, a \emph{symbolic} linear solve
whose cost grows explosively with the number of coordinates. A wall attributable
to one interior operation is a fact about that operation, not about the library.

The study's own adapter anticipated this. Its docstring records that a second
mode exists and that ``the gap between them is a measure of how much the
documented path costs a user relative to the informed one.'' That secondary
result was never measured. This report measures it, and then --- because
measuring it for one contestant and not the others would be an asymmetry
favouring the author's own engine --- repeats the exercise for MechanicsDSL.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Method}
% ===========================================================================

Each engine is driven along several paths through its own public interface, on
the same $N$-link chain, under the same wall clock, and adjudicated against the
same closed-form reference of \code{reference.py}. Nothing frozen is altered:
no case is removed, no recorded figure is restated, and the engine under test
remains pinned at \code{a8dc2b2}. This is harness growth, which the freeze
permits.

\paragraph{Correctness gates every timing.}
A faster wrong answer is worth nothing, and three of the four faults recorded in
this study's own apparatus (TR-2026-04, Table~1) were convention mismatches that
produced clean constant error ratios. Every variant is therefore validated
against the reference at $16$ probe states before its build time is allowed to
count. A variant that is fast and disagrees is reported as \textsc{disagree},
not as a success. \textbf{No variant disagreed.}

\paragraph{A timing caveat.}
The machine used for this report ran roughly twice as slowly as the one that
produced the frozen sweep: the unmodified MechanicsDSL path reached $8$ links
here against $12$ in the record. Every comparison below is therefore
same-session, variant against variant. The archived figures are not restated
from this run and remain as recorded.

\noindent Neither remedy suffices alone, and this is the informative part.
Kane's method by itself buys nothing --- $6$ links, the same as idiomatic, and
slower at $N=6$. Deferring the solve and applying CSE buys two links, $6$ to
$8$. Together they reach $30$. The two compound rather than add, because the
numeric solve is what removes the \code{LUsolve} blow-up, and only once that is
gone does Kane's cheaper mass matrix govern the cost.

\textbf{Driven as its documentation presents it, SymPy reaches $6$. Driven by a
user who knows the library, it reaches $30$.}

% ===========================================================================
\section{MechanicsDSL: the wall was one cosmetic call}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Two hypotheses, both wrong}
MechanicsDSL was never subject to SymPy's disease. At
\code{NUMERIC\_MASS\_MATRIX\_MIN\_COORDS = 4} (\code{compiler.py:69}) it already
returns \code{\{"\_\_mass\_matrix\_\_": (M, F)\}} and defers the $M^{-1}$ solve
to evaluation time. The remedy that carried SymPy from $8$ to $30$ was in place
here from the start.

The remaining candidate was CSE: \code{solver/core.py} lambdified $M$ and $F$
without it, and the chain's mass matrix has $O(N^2)$ entries built from cosines
of angle differences. It made matters worse --- $107$\,s against $86.5$\,s at
$N=8$ --- because CSE's own analysis costs time and lambdify was not where the
time was.

\subsection{What the profile showed}
Profiling the $N=8$ compile settled it:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrl@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Location} & \textbf{Share} & \textbf{Nature} \\
\midrule
\code{sympy.simplify} & $97\%$ & cosmetic rewriting \\
\quad $\to$ \code{trigsimp.\_futrig} & --- & \\
\quad $\to$ \code{polytools.factor} & --- & multivariate polynomial factorisation \\
\quad $\to$ \code{densebasic.py} (self time) & $48\%$ & \\
\quad $\to$ \code{densearith.py} (self time) & $24\%$ & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Simplification rewrites an expression into an equivalent one. It
changes the \emph{size} of the derived equations and never their \emph{value}.
The engine was spending almost all of its compile budget on legibility.

\subsection{The measurement}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrl@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Variant} & \textbf{$N=8$ build} & \textbf{Reach} & \textbf{Relative error} \\
\midrule
\code{frozen}     & $86.5$\,s & $8$  & $2.1663226767998367\times10^{-14}$ \\
\code{cse}        & $107.0$\,s & $8$  & $2.1663226767998367\times10^{-14}$ \\
\code{fastsimp}   & $88.5$\,s & $8$  & $2.1663226767998367\times10^{-14}$ \\
\code{nosimplify} & $\mathbf{2.04}$\,\textbf{s} & $\mathbf{30}$ & $2.1663226767998367\times10^{-14}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The error is identical to the last digit across all four. At $N=30$
the compile takes $112$\,s. Past that the engine \emph{refuses}, raising
\code{RecursionError} and returning \code{success: False} --- a loud failure,
not a wrong answer, and the correct behaviour when it cannot proceed. But the
reason it could not proceed turned out not to be its own.

\subsection{The second limit was CPython's, not the engine's}
Deriving a strongly coupled system walks expression trees whose depth grows
with the number of coordinates, and past roughly thirty the planar chain
exceeds CPython's default recursion limit of $1000$. That default, not the
mathematics and not the engine, is what refused $N=32$.

Raising the limit --- necessarily together with the stack, since a raised limit
on a default stack converts a catchable \code{RecursionError} into a hard
interpreter crash --- changes an instant refusal into a completed derivation:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrl@{}}
\toprule
$N$ & \textbf{Build} & \textbf{Relative error} & \textbf{Note} \\
\midrule
$30$ & $112$\,s & $9.76\times10^{-14}$ & default limit suffices \\
$32$ & $145$\,s & $9.13\times10^{-14}$ & \emph{refuses} at the default limit \\
$36$ & $181$\,s & $1.19\times10^{-13}$ & \\
$40$ & $279$\,s & $1.64\times10^{-13}$ & \\
$48$ & $467$\,s & $2.38\times10^{-13}$ & \\
$60$ & $916$\,s & $4.09\times10^{-13}$ & \\
$80$ & $2338$\,s & $5.95\times10^{-13}$ & \\
$100$ & $4414$\,s & $1.28\times10^{-12}$ & measurement stopped here \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Cost grows as roughly $O(N^3)$: doubling $30$ to $60$ costs $8.2\times$
the time, and the $N=100$ figure of $4414$\,s came within two per cent of
the extrapolation made from $N=60$. The residual grows far more slowly, from
$9.8\times10^{-14}$ at $N=30$ to $1.3\times10^{-12}$ at $N=100$, which is the
expected accumulation as the mass matrix's condition number rises with $N$ and
remains four orders of magnitude inside the $10^{-8}$ adjudication tolerance.

\textbf{The engine derives a 100-coordinate system correctly.} \emph{No ceiling was
found.} The ladder was stopped at $100$ because the next rung would have taken
about two hours, not because the engine failed or slowed abnormally. The
eventual limit is expected to be memory rather than time --- $10^4$ mass-matrix
entries are held as expression trees simultaneously --- but that is a
prediction, not a measurement.

\section{A control that reported working while doing nothing}
% ===========================================================================

The engine already intended to skip this cost. Seven call sites wrapped
simplification in a deadline and, on expiry, logged ``using unsimplified
equation'' and continued. The intent was never delivered, for two independent
reasons.

\paragraph{First: the deadline could not interrupt the work.}
\code{config.simplification\_timeout} was implemented with a watchdog thread. A
Python thread cannot interrupt SymPy inside a C-level routine, which is exactly
where expensive simplification spends its time. Setting the deadline to
$0.05$\,s made the expiry message appear and left the compile at $88.5$\,s.

\paragraph{Second: the expiry was raised where nobody could catch it.}
On Windows the handler executed \code{raise TimeoutError(...)} \emph{inside the
timer thread}. An exception raised there cannot propagate to the caller: it
printed a traceback to \code{stderr}, killed the timer thread, and left every
\code{except TimeoutError} around the call sites permanently unreachable. A
\code{threading.Event} was set and never read. The deadline was inert and noisy
at once.

\paragraph{Third: the off switch selected the slowest path.}
The guard read \code{if config.simplification\_timeout > 0}, with an
\code{else} branch calling \code{sp.simplify} unguarded. Setting the value to
$0$ --- the value a user reaches for to turn a timeout off --- did not disable
simplification. It removed the deadline and let simplification run unbounded.

\paragraph{Fourth: an interpreter default presented as a derivation failure.}
The \code{RecursionError} surfaced as ``Equation derivation failed: maximum
recursion depth exceeded'', which reads as the engine finding the system
intractable. It was CPython's default limit. A user meeting that message has no
indication that the system is well within reach and that one setting would
admit it.

\medskip
\noindent This is not an instance of the study's central claim: no wrong number
was returned, and the engine's physics was never affected. It is the same shape
one level down --- a control that reports success and has no effect --- and it
was found in the author's own engine by the same discipline of consulting an
artefact rather than reasoning further.

% ===========================================================================
\section{The fix}
% ===========================================================================

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0.35em]
  \item \textbf{A real switch.} \code{config.enable\_simplification} (default
        \code{True}, preserving existing behaviour) skips simplification
        outright. This is the only reliable way to avoid the cost.
  \item \textbf{One policy, one place.} All seven call sites now route through
        \code{utils/simplification.py:maybe\_simplify()}, which owns the
        decision, never propagates a simplification failure, and names the
        quantity in its warnings.
  \item \textbf{An honest deadline.} The Windows timer now sets its flag in the
        thread and raises on the \emph{caller's} thread after the body returns.
        The expiry is catchable and the \code{stderr} tracebacks are gone. It
        remains advisory, and both the docstring and the warning now say so.
  \item \textbf{The trap removed.} Passing $0$ warns that it removes the
        deadline rather than the simplification, and points at
        \code{enable\_simplification}.
  \item \textbf{Deep recursion.} A \code{RecursionError} in derivation now
        retries once with a raised limit on an enlarged stack
        (\code{utils/recursion.py}), governed by \code{config.deep\_recursion}
        (default on), \code{recursion\_limit} and \code{derivation\_stack\_mb}.
        Only the derivation is retried, not the whole compile, so parsing is
        not repeated. If every stack size is refused the retry runs inline
        rather than failing. The residual error message now states that the
        limit was the interpreter's and names the settings.
  \item \textbf{A latent defect fixed on the way.} Running derivation on a
        worker thread would have broken the Unix deadline outright:
        \code{signal.alarm} is main-thread only and raises \code{ValueError}
        elsewhere. \code{timeout()} now detects that case and degrades to the
        advisory behaviour instead of crashing.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Measured through the supported switch, on one machine in one session:
$N=5$, $14.15\to1.10$\,s ($12.9\times$); $N=8$, $112.8\to3.05$\,s
($37.0\times$). Accelerations bit-identical in both.

\paragraph{This fix is not in the study's engine.}
It lives on \code{fix/simplification-cost}. \code{FREEZE.md} is explicit that if
the engine moves off \code{a8dc2b2} the baseline is void, and the frozen figures
were measured on the unfixed engine. The study's pin stands, and the \code{v2.1.3}
tag keeps it recoverable.

\section{The same question, the same budget, to all three}\label{sec:equal}
% ===========================================================================

Establishing that MechanicsDSL reaches $100$ while leaving SymPy and Drake at
the $30$ where the frozen ladder happened to stop would have replaced one
asymmetry with another, and this one would favour the author's own engine.
Neither of the other two failed at $30$; neither was asked for more.

Every engine was therefore asked for the same systems, each along the best
path found for it, under the same wall clock, in one process on one machine,
adjudicated against the same reference. Drake has no native Windows build, so
the comparison was made in WSL where all three import together --- otherwise
engine and operating system would be confounded.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrlrlrl@{}}
\toprule
& \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{Drake}} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{SymPy}} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{MechanicsDSL}} \\
\cmidrule(lr){2-3}\cmidrule(lr){4-5}\cmidrule(lr){6-7}
$N$ & time & residual & time & residual & time & residual \\
\midrule
$40$ & $0.3$\,s & $1.7\times10^{-10}$ & $74.3$\,s & $3.6\times10^{-13}$ & $145.0$\,s & $1.7\times10^{-13}$ \\
$60$ & $0.2$\,s & $4.1\times10^{-10}$ & $280.2$\,s & $7.1\times10^{-13}$ & $468.0$\,s & $4.2\times10^{-13}$ \\
$80$ & $0.2$\,s & $9.9\times10^{-10}$ & $693.3$\,s & $1.2\times10^{-12}$ & $1100.9$\,s & $5.9\times10^{-13}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent \textbf{All three reach $80$ coordinates. No engine failed at any rung,
and no engine disagreed with the reference.} Reach, which the frozen sweep
reported as a three-tier separation, is not a differentiator between these
engines at all.

Three things in this table are worth stating precisely, and two of them are
unfavourable to the author's engine.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.3em]
  \item \textbf{Drake is in a different class.} Its cost is flat at
        $0.2$--$0.3$\,s across the whole ladder --- an $O(N)$ articulated-body
        algorithm against two symbolic approaches growing as roughly $O(N^3)$.
        At $N=80$ that is a factor of some $5500$. Its residual is larger
        ($10^{-9}$ against $10^{-13}$) but remains far inside tolerance.
  \item \textbf{Driven well, SymPy is faster than MechanicsDSL at every
        rung} --- $74$ against $145$\,s, $280$ against $468$\,s, $693$
        against $1101$\,s, consistently about $1.6\times$. Of the two symbolic
        engines the author's is the slower.
  \item \textbf{MechanicsDSL returns the smaller residuals on this family},
        by roughly a factor of two at every rung, and \textbf{that advantage does
        not survive a change of mechanism.} On the slider--crank SymPy leads
        (median $4.8\times10^{-16}$ against $7.5\times10^{-16}$); on the cart--pole
        MechanicsDSL has the worst case of the three ($1.0\times10^{-12}$).
        The lead is a property of the planar chain rather than of the
        formulation and should not be claimed generally.

        A caution attaches to the cart--pole column, and it is an instance of
        the diagnostic of \S6: SymPy's residual there is \emph{exactly} zero
        across 144 probes. That is not accuracy. SymPy derives the identical
        mass matrix $\left[\begin{smallmatrix} M{+}m & ml\cos\theta \\ ml\cos\theta & ml^2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ to
        the hand reference and then performs the same \texttt{numpy} solve on the
        same inputs, so the arithmetic is bit-identical and the residual measures
        nothing. The derivations agreeing exactly is a real and strong result;
        the zero cannot rank the engines, and \textbf{no accuracy ordering should
        be read from that column at all.}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Provenance.}
Every row above comes from one engine state in one session on one platform.
The MechanicsDSL ladder of \S4.4 does not: its rows were taken across three
code states as the fixes here were developed, and WSL runs roughly twice as
fast as Windows for this work ($N=80$: $1101$\,s here against $2338$\,s
there). The two tables must not be mixed. Where they differ, this one governs.

\section{Consequences for the study}
% ===========================================================================

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Engine} & \textbf{As driven (frozen)} & \textbf{Equal budget} & \textbf{Time at $N=80$} \\
\midrule
\code{sympy.physics.mechanics} & $5$  & $\mathbf{80}$ & $693$\,s \\
MechanicsDSL                   & $12$ & $\mathbf{80}$ & $1101$\,s \\
Drake                          & $30$ & $\mathbf{80}$ & $\mathbf{0.2}$\,\textbf{s} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent \textbf{The three-tier capability claim does not survive, and nothing
replaces it.} Given the same systems and the same clock, all three engines
answer every rung to $80$ coordinates. The tiers reported in the frozen sweep
measured how each engine happened to be driven, and $80$ is where the ladder
stopped rather than where any engine failed.

What separates the engines is speed, and there the ordering is unambiguous.
Drake's $O(N)$ recursive algorithm is flat across the ladder and beats both
symbolic engines by some $5500\times$ at $N=80$; that is a difference of
algorithm class rather than of engineering quality, and no change of driving
affects it. Between the two symbolic engines, SymPy driven well is about
$1.6\times$ faster than MechanicsDSL at every rung. MechanicsDSL returns
residuals about twice as small \emph{on this family}, an advantage that reverses
on the cart--pole and the slider--crank and so should not be generalised.
\textbf{On the axis the frozen sweep was read as separating them, they do not
separate.}


Two asymmetries must be reported together, because they point in opposite
directions and quoting either alone would mislead.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.3em]
  \item \textbf{Against MechanicsDSL.} SymPy's $30$ is reachable by any
        competent user reading the documentation. MechanicsDSL's $30$ required
        a fix to the engine; before it, no documented means existed, because the
        one control provided did not work.
  \item \textbf{For MechanicsDSL.} Its ceiling was never architectural.
        It already avoided the symbolic solve that limited SymPy, and neither
        limit it did have was inherent: one accidental cost in a cosmetic
        call, and one interpreter default. Both are now removed, and the
        engine derives systems more than three times the size of anything the
        frozen sweep asked of any engine.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{One default still stands in the way.}
The $100$-coordinate result requires \code{enable\_simplification = False},
which is off by default. Out of the box a user still meets the simplification
cost long before the recursion limit. That default was deliberately left alone:
changing it would alter the derived equations of every existing system from tidy
to bulky, which is a visible behavioural change and a release decision rather
than a bug fix. Disabling simplification automatically above some coordinate
count is the obvious follow-up.

\paragraph{What the paper must say instead.}
The abstract's ``chains of $5$, $12$ and $30$ links respectively'' and
\S1.6's ``factor-of-six capability difference'' both state as engine properties
what are properties of the driving. The defensible claim is narrower and more
interesting: \emph{reach depends on the path taken through the tool, the
documented path was the slowest of the four measured for SymPy, and the
capability tiers reported in the frozen sweep dissolve when each engine is
driven by an informed user.}

\paragraph{What is unchanged.}
Every correctness finding stands. Across $50$ measurements in this report ---
four SymPy variants and five MechanicsDSL variants over two ladders --- there
were \textbf{zero disagreements with the closed-form reference}. No engine
returned a wrong answer while reporting success, under any driving style, at any
$N$. Claim~(A) of the study is untouched and claim~(B) remains unsupported.
